Can we detect whether transplanted inhibitory neurons truly integrate into the brain's circuitry?

Your brain runs on two kinds of signals: neurons that say "go" (excitation) and neurons that say "stop" (inhibition). A seizure happens when "go" overwhelms "stop", like a car with a strong accelerator and weak brakes.

In drug-resistant epilepsy (about 30% of people with epilepsy), medication doesn't fix this. One promising therapy transplants new inhibitory neurons, called GABAergic interneurons, into the circuit to act as fresh brakes and restore the balance. It has reached early first-in-human trials.

Here's the catch: a transplanted cell only helps if it actually plugs into the circuit and connects to the right neurons. We can't watch that happen inside a living brain. All we have is noisy, indirect data: EEG, brain imaging and molecular markers.

The question I'm trying to resolve: did the transplanted cells integrate and form the right connections? To answer it, I model the circuit as a graph (cells are dots, connections are lines) and train a Graph Neural Network to read the data. The model has to show its reasoning, so the answer can be checked, not just believed.

Solution: model the circuit as a graph and build the explanation methods into the GNN itself, so every prediction comes with its own reasoning (attention, saliency, concepts), then test whether that reasoning matches known circuit biology

Comparing the model's reasoning with known biology is called the "validation" phase (the crux of the project: an explanation that looks right but is wrong is worse than an honest black box)

Next, for each prediction, we:

  1. train the GNN on open epilepsy datasets (OpenNeuro IDEAS, EBRAINS)
  2. record attention weights, saliency maps and concept scores
  3. list what known biology says the model should attend to, e.g. GABAergic inhibitory connections when predicting excitatory/inhibitory rebalancing
  4. compare the model's stated reasoning with that list
  5. if they match => the model is right for the right reasons
  6. if not => the model is picking up spurious correlations, and we flag it
  7. publish the pipeline, model and validation method openly

Benefits:

  • a readout of whether the circuit repaired, with reasons a clinician can question
  • a wrong explanation gets caught, not hidden
  • open pipeline, reusable for other high-stakes graph-based clinical AI

Why epilepsy:

  • about 30% of the 50 million people with epilepsy don't respond to drugs
  • inhibitory interneuron transplants, which aim to restore the excitatory/inhibitory balance, have reached first-in-human trials
  • the open problem: did the transplanted cells integrate and connect correctly?

Risks:

  • scarce data (open datasets weren't built for stem-cell integration, so validation may use proxy or synthetic data)
  • the validation may return an ambiguous or negative result, which is still a useful, publishable answer
